What Happens at the Lab?

We live in a time when there's endless information about money and investing. You could say we're drowning in it.

So if the information is already at our fingertips, why do so many of us still hold back when it comes to doing something with our money, like investing it?

Maybe what we're missing is the confidence to act, even when the information is there.

The Money Research Lab started with one question. Why do smart, educated, independent women who know they need to do something with their money still hold back from taking the first step?

To find answers, the Lab goes out and meets as many women as it can. Through those conversations, it tries to understand what we think and feel, and what gets in the way between wanting to do something with our money and actually doing it.

If we can break that gap down, find the barriers, and give them names, we can start building tools and solutions around what women actually need.

Remember how Waze asked drivers to help map the roads? The Lab works on a similar idea. We're mapping how women think, feel, and make decisions about money and investing.

And that's where you come in.

The Lab needs your profile. Your story, your questions, what you're weighing up, what's holding you back, and what's worked for you. Maybe you've already started investing. Maybe you're still on the fence.

As more women share their stories, we can hear more points of view, spot patterns, and test ideas that could become tools to help women go from knowing they need to do something to actually doing it.

The more women who build wealth of their own and get to choose how they live, the better off the world will be.
Behind the Lab

I'm Moran

I'm Moran Shaked, a product designer (UX/UI), a mom of two, and a die-hard Excel fan.

I'm no economist, financier, or investment adviser. But for more than a decade, I've been trying to understand why women don't engage with our money the way we can, the way we should.

It started in 2013, when I went to Israel's first Bitcoin hackathon. I was the only woman in the room. I pitched an idea and my team took first place. One question stayed with me long after the prize.

Where were all the women?

Since then, I've founded Miss Bitcoin, wrote Financial Candy, run workshops, talked to women, done research, tried things, failed, and learned.

I believe money is so much more than a number in a bank account. It's the freedom to choose how we live, what we do, where we work, when we leave, and what we build for ourselves.

I call it “the business of opening up options.”

I want to open up the option first.
Then we'll see what we do with it.

I want women to build enough wealth of their own that they don't have to stay somewhere that isn't good for them, that they can go after their dreams, and above all, that they get to choose.

After more than a decade of questions, experiments, and projects, I started The Money Research Lab to keep looking for answers. This time, I want to do it systematically, with the women I'm building for.

Building the Lab Together

One of the Lab's goals is to turn what we learn into tools built around how women think and make decisions about money and investing.

The Lab wants to build those tools with the women who take part in the research. They'll help decide what's worth building, try things out, and see what works for them.

The women in the study are part of the Lab's DNA.

Listening

We start with what happens in real life. The questions, the doubts, the things that get in the way, and the decisions we make, including the ones we put off again and again.

The Lab goes out to meet women. It listens to what they say and how they say it. It hears the hesitation, notices where things get stuck, and asks another question when it needs to.

Because a thought that keeps coming up for one woman might be a clue to what another hundred are experiencing.

Mapping

When you start connecting the dots, a picture begins to emerge.

What gets in the way again and again? Where do we get stuck? What do the women who took the first step have in common? What's happening for those of us still on the fence?

The Lab maps what it hears and starts to see the patterns underneath.

Making Sense of It

How do we turn what we learn into action?

Each of us has our own operating system when it comes to money. Our ways of thinking, habits, and barriers shape how we decide.

The Lab wants to use what it learns about that operating system to design tools around how we make decisions, so the products we build can better serve our financial interests. The women in the study will help test ideas, give feedback, and shape tools around what they actually need.

What the Lab Studies

The Lab studies the gap between “I know I should” and the moment we actually take the first step. What keeps us on the fence? What gets us moving? What patterns of thinking shape those decisions?

The goal is to understand what's holding us back and build tools that help us turn intention into action.
Meet the Personas

The Lab mapped different kinds of users to explore the ways we think about money and investing and how we handle the decisions that come with them.

Each persona gives us a chance to recognize ourselves, see what's driving us, and understand what might help us move forward.

When we understand how we work, it's easier to find a way to take the next step that fits us.
Why You?

You're exactly who the Lab wants to hear from.

You want to start investing. You know it makes sense. Maybe you've already read up on it and looked into your options, but something is still holding you back. Time passes and that first step still doesn't happen.

You don't need to be an expert or know “enough” about investing. What you've experienced, what you're unsure about, and what's holding you back are all part of what the Lab wants to learn.

The more women who take part, the better we can understand the million-dollar question. Why do we hold back even when we know it makes sense to act? And what could help us move from worrying about taking action to doing something that serves our own financial interests?

Your first step can be as simple as talking to the Lab.

A Little About Me

I'm Moran

I'm Moran, a product designer (UX/UI), a mom of two, a partner to the man I call the Mister, and a die-hard Excel fan.

I'm no economist, financier, or investment adviser. I actually studied visual communication at Shenkar. But for more than a decade, I've been trying to understand why women don't engage with our money the way we can, the way we should. Mostly, I want to know what could help us go from knowing we need to do something to actually doing it.

In 2013, I went to Israel's first Bitcoin hackathon. . I was the only woman in the room, and the idea I brought ended up winning first place. Without meaning to, I'd got myself a front-row seat to something historic, taking shape right in front of me. And I kept asking myself the same question.

Where were all the women?

Miss Bitcoin

That's where Miss Bitcoin came from. It started as a community where I mapped women working in Bitcoin around the world. Then it became a project to make that world more accessible to women without a tech background.

The project didn't take off, but it got me thinking about something bigger. Long before Bitcoin entered the conversation, many women were already missing from conventional investing, from the capital markets.

That sent me on a whole journey of conversations with women, research, and questions that don't always make it into the generic surveys from fancy research institutes.

Financial Candy

Along the way, I also started Financial Candy. I felt like every finance site was talking over my head, using terms I didn't understand and language that didn't speak to me. I wanted to make something I'd actually want to read. Real-life finance with a little style. A small piece of Financial Candy you could take with you, whether you were waiting in line or riding the train.

Over the years, I organized all sorts of things, from pension workshops in my living room that ran until midnight to big collaborations and ideas that never took off. Mostly, I helped other people build their products.

Now I want to build something that really matters to me.

I want to figure out which tools will help us get off the fence and do something in our own best interests, and ours alone.

And that's how The Money Research Lab was born.

How I Ended Up at Israel's First Bitcoin Hackathon

In 2013, I was trying to figure things out. After a tough breakup, I was wondering where I was headed professionally and how I could build a different financial future for myself. Somewhere in my searching, I came across an announcement for Israel's first Bitcoin hackathon, a competition for developers. I barely knew what Bitcoin was, but when I read about it I felt it in my bones. Something big was happening and I had to be there.

I saw a list of about 70 developers, and there was me, a designer from a completely different world. I gave myself a two-point brief. I had to show up with an idea, and I couldn't go home just because I felt out of place. It was 48 hours, goddammit.

When it was time to pitch, I got shy. I waited until the end, and when they asked if anyone had another idea, I nearly let it go. In the end, I pitched an idea for a real problem at the time. It was hard to buy and transfer Bitcoin. A team formed around the idea, we built it together, and we won first place. The prize included two bitcoin for each team member, back when they were worth a lot less, and a trading prize on eToro.

What stayed with me was the feeling that I'd landed a front-row seat. Right in front of me were people building and shaping a new economy. I looked around and asked myself:

How is it possible that there are so few of us here? Money, investing, and the ability to take part in the financial world are a must-have for us.

I went there to find out what Bitcoin was. I came away with a much bigger question about women and money.
